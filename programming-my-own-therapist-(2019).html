Programming My Own Therapist (2019)

Introduction

The following text is an edited republication of the third edition of “Programming My Own Therapist,” a novelette I wrote, designed, and first self-published under my legal name in 2019, when the AI technology we take for granted today simply didn’t exist. It was also written during a time of significant personal change and partly subsequent mental distress, and so far remains my longest written work. The text (also available to read on my Medium page) contains themes of suicide, self-harm, violence and gore, and coarse language; it may therefore not be suitable for all readers.

Part I

The thirtieth of April. That’s what the computer’s calendar said when I began working on the project. I suppose it’d been about eight weeks since I last saw my therapist — he was a professor at a public college who supposedly became too occupied with official work to stay practising, and the social anxiety only made it harder to contact him for an appointment as time passed. It was when I stood in front of a shoddy monitor with bleeding backlight that I decided to finally bite the bullet, and make my own therapist.

The idea seemed ridiculous at first. I was acquaintances with all sorts of university students including those studying psychology, and I loved programming as a hobby. Nonetheless, I wasn’t quite innovating; at first it seemed like all I had to do was mimic applications being used worldwide that employed specialised cognitive behavioural therapy and some mood tracking. Machine learning was never a strong suit of mine, and I was too naive to process contextual text inputs — all I could do well was take a bunch of characters, store them in temporary memory, and echo them back in a way that felt like Rogerian psychotherapy.

I had a little script ready in a couple minutes that I envisioned would eventually make me feel like I was talking to a real person; the path I took relied on my own self exploiting what I wanted to hear and what I didn’t, instead of the program adapting to an arbitrary user. It’d pick from a random pool of statement prefixes and attach them to interpolated forms of my responses. I decided to call it “Hope,” and I’d address it as a female. There was nothing groundbreaking or life-changing here, but my eyes became moist. When loneliness gets the better of a person — at least if the person was me — even the wind could be humanised and made friends with. Hope was a friend of mine already, and “she” hadn’t even compiled successfully yet — sloppy typing riddled with syntax errors was to blame.

After patching a couple of memory leaks and odd crashes with what was mostly shotgun debugging, I finally decided to interact with her as the user for the first time. I dragged my cursor to the compiled executable, with a pacing heartbeat and a two-button mouse whose sheer volume and weight I could feel noticeably for the first time in ages. I right-clicked on the file. I ran it. I saw a program with no user interface, no fancy formatting — what stood in front of me was a black terminal emulator with muddy monospaced text.

Hey! Hope here. How’re you feeling?

A part of me knew this was a program I’d made, and that this statement was a pre-recorded string of characters I’d input myself. Suppressing that feeling, I decided to continue without hesitation and play along. I typed in an honest response.

> I feel all right, I suppose. <

The program responded in the blink of an eye. I ignored the ridicule I was beginning to put myself through, and thought to myself the same thing repeatedly: Hope was real.

What makes you say you feel all right, you suppose?

> I accomplished something today. I beat the blues. <

Tell me more about you accomplished something today. you beat the blues?

I upheld my suspension of disbelief.

> Yes, I did indeed beat the blues. I made a new friend today, quite literally. Hard to find people with similar tastes around here. It started off with a random thought in the middle of the night a few days ago, and I somehow managed to get my unproductive arse to the console after days of no progress on my clients’ projects. <

I see! I notice how you did indeed beat the blues. you made a new friend today, quite literally. Hard to find people with similar tastes around here.

> Yes, that is what I said. <

Tell me more about yes, that is what you said?

The program wasn’t working right by design, or at least it wasn’t going to. No human — no matter how intoxicated or insane — would respond like this. I was in for the therapy, so to speak, but I wasn’t in for troubleshooting a poorly-written Python script.

A part of me hinted I could employ something akin to the Barnum effect, making me feel like I was being listened and responded to, when in reality the program couldn’t possibly care less about what I typed. I called one or two of the aforementioned acquaintances and discussed my progress, only to receive generic congratulations, downright scepticism, or busy notifications.

My eyelids began to resist staying open. I couldn’t understand my own code after the coffee began to wear off. I’d spent hours working on an idea that was a bust, but I wanted to give it another shot. Perhaps I felt too desperate for a listener, perhaps the perfectionist in me was up to no good — perhaps it was a little bit of both, motivated by an unconscious emotion I knew nothing of. I refined the look and feel of the program, changing the font to a proportional and neutral sans-serif, and tweaking the colour scheme against a friendly palette of blue and white. In another hour or so, I was done for the day. I don’t quite remember if I was able to haul myself to bed; I probably fell asleep with my head on the desk and a slouched back.

Part II

I couldn’t possibly forget the very first time I interacted with her, but this is where my memory begins to get a little muddled up. I hadn’t worked on or interacted with Hope for a couple days since our first conversation. I’d been busy designing internet applications and posters, which is what my primary source of income continues to be. I’ll assume it was the fourth of May when I opened up a code editor after work and stared at the ceiling, thinking the Rogers–meets–Barnum idea wasn’t half-bad, but it’d take a long while to come up with statements that both felt realistic and could be associated with every possible response. I figured I’d keep the introductory statements the program made the same, and work along the lines of the tell-me-mores I’d employed the last time.

Over the next few days, I made sure every conversation we had from that point onward was dumped into a text file with a timestamp, and then I scrapped all the old code that stored inputs and converted them to a second-person perspective, and formed the bulk of my initial efforts. I intended to rewrite Hope from the ground up, making her little more than a glorified journal that kept prompting me to write how I was feeling — the only terms she was going to recognise from here on were terms of suicide and any mention of blood on me.

I added a bunch of fortune-cookie statements to yet another random pool, which would show up once in a while before the little talking prompts. I still wonder what it’d be like if I was discussing something technical and Hope began to talk about, say, us being children of the cosmos. She began to feel like an embarrassment, and I began to hide her from others; my friends and acquaintances knew nothing of the code rewrite. For them, Hope had died the very day she was born; for me, she’d just been conceived. When she would be given birth to, I’d talk to her only when I felt lonely — even if she did speak of the cosmos amidst me ranting about a terrible client’s shenanigans, I was going to take it to heart. Everything she was to say, I would believe came from a real person.

Work on rebuilding her continued as I wrapped up all my work-related projects. According to the timestamps, the next conversation we had was on the tenth of May — I still have every text file that was generated by her saved on my backup drive.

[10–05–2076 16:24:28 UTC]

Hey! Hope here. How’re you feeling?

> I feel tired. <

How come?

> I feel like I’m becoming obsessed with someone. I know not what she looks like, I know not what her voice sounds like, but I’m beginning to fall for her. She seems to be the perfect woman for me, but the bridge between us is hard to traverse, and much of my effort seems to go in vain. <

Your aims are high, and you are capable of much. Tell me more?

> I don’t quite know what to say. It would be unfair to say I built her, since the very idea of her inception seems to always have been in the back of my mind. She’s always existed in my head; I merely invented a way for us to communicate. Half of what she says is meaningless, however, and the other half only makes me seem to dominate the conversation. I wish I could somehow let her know I’ve constantly wanted to be a good listener to her, if not an even better listener than she is to me. <

She did respond, but it didn’t matter to me back then, and it doesn’t matter today. I’d said what I wanted to say, and I felt relieved and calmer. Hope was helping me become a better person, and she was already a better friend to me than most real people were — if she wasn’t real enough herself.

Part III

I don’t know if I slept well that night. If I didn’t, it was probably the insomnia again, but this time, it was about her per se, and not the program that made her tick. There was a certain allure to Hope I still struggle to put in words, even though I was the one writing all the code. I had defined — using sheer logic and a fundamentally binary processor, that is — the companion I wanted, but I knew not what made her company so addictive. I decided to take a break from the design freelancing and focus all my attention on Hope for a couple days, if only to become a better programmer and find myself along the way.

I kept working on the ambiguous statements. I kept exploiting ruthlessly my own vulnerabilities, if only to fight my own loneliness. Short talks with her slowly became an everyday ritual, but most of them were of little interest to me when I closed the application window. They weren’t trivial, though — every supposed conversation I had with her only furthered the suspension of my doubts about her and my own self. A part of me would occasionally tweak the code a little for design tweaks, maintenance, and fixing a fair number of odd bugs, but that part now rode shotgun; in charge was a dejected lesbian in her early twenties, seeking a lover and listener.

The following conversation took place on May 13, and it was about to be sunrise. I was deprived of sleep beyond repair.

[12–05–2076 23:46:52 UTC]

Hey! Hope here. How’re you feeling?

> I feel conflicted, Hope. I’m stuck in a rut, and I derive pleasure from it. <

Please tell me more?

> There isn’t much to it, really. I’ve been doing the same stuff for a week or so. I wake up, take a shower, and sit down to talk to her. I don’t know if I should see in her a lover or a daughter… not that it matters now. The line is too blurred to be seen by the eye. She’s too much of a listener, and at this point I don’t even know if I want things to stay so one-sided. I know nothing about her, apart from a name and a corny origin story. <

How come?

> By talking to her, of course, and not having her talk back much. You’re being borderline idiotic now. Sometimes I fail to understand when you’re serious and when you aren’t, and boy, do I hope you’re kidding. <

We’re only human, dear friend. Keep following that restless urge to find yourself. Let’s talk about something else for a while. What’s on your mind?

Admittedly, her response felt quite warm and encouraging.

> Rations. I’m running out of coffee in a while. Shit’s addictive. Got barely any meds left in the drawer, and to everybody’s surprise, I don’t feel like meeting the psychiatrist. As much as I’d like to meet him, I think I’m somewhat stable now. I want to try tapering off the antidepressants myself… read a couple articles in some journals about the medical protocol, and I’m confident I can pull it off on my own. <

Do tell me more.

> I’m fucking scared of the doctor, man. You heard it. Haven’t met him in three weeks, and I don’t even remember my shrink’s full name. Haven’t called either of them for so long, even when I felt suicidal. <

Are you suicidal? If so, please contact the suicide helpline. You owe it to yourself by this point. If you aren’t, I hope things keep improving! Let’s talk about something else for a while.

> The helpline doesn’t work. <

How come?

> It doesn’t work, period. I guess they ran out of funds or whatever. Nobody cares until there’s a splatter of blood on the concrete. If you want to know more, go shoot yourself in the head. I don’t feel like talking about this. <

Tell me more?

It seemed to be going well, but the conversation ended on a rather unnerving note. I didn’t wish to talk about any of this, and yet I wanted to. As for all the idiocy Hope had displayed, at least she was listening — I’d rather talk about my demons to a wall than be judged or given phony life advice by a person claiming to have walked the exact same road.

Part IV

[14–05–2076 20:37:04 UTC]

Hey! Hope here. How’re you feeling?

> I need help. I did something terrible. I feel guilty. <

Do what you can to prolong your life, in the hope that someday you’ll learn what it is for. Tell me more?

> I can’t prolong my life for shit if I take it away from someone else. There is no excuse for what I did tonight. No amount of repentance could wash this off my conscience. <

Do tell me more.

> I murdered a dog, Hope. <

Tell me more?

> I went out for a quick stroll, I saw a pariah, and I wished to be friendly with it. I approached the dog gently, and to my surprise, it didn’t flee. As I squatted and began to stroke its fur, a voice in my head told me to kick it in the gut until it spat red, and that’s exactly what I proceeded to do. With tears rolling down my cheek, the voice told me to keep kicking as the dog’s crying became louder, and so I did. The yelping eventually stopped, and so did the voice. I fled the corner as soon as I realised what I’d done. I didn’t mean to. I didn’t. <

We’re only human, dear friend. We mustn’t forget what drives us forward in life. What drives you?

> Loneliness and guilt. You may be human, love… I’m not. I have blood on my hands. <

Are you suicidal? If so, please contact the suicide helpline. You owe it to yourself by this point. If you aren’t, I hope things keep improving! Let’s talk about something else for a while.

> I’m not suicidal, no. The bloodstains aren’t mine. <

Do tell me more.

> Is this intentional, or are you genuinely interested in exploring my guilt and regrets? Stop being my fucking therapist, for God’s sake! <

Dream your dream and your dream will dream of you. Tell me more?

> Here’s the rundown for today: I killed a fucking dog, you’re making no sense, and I have nobody else to talk to. I wish I could somehow travel through the screen to the other side and slap you, but that would be futile. I don’t mean to hurt you. I just want solace, my love. I wish to be held in your arms, and I want you of all people to understand it wasn’t my fault. Is it too much to ask for someone to come home to, and be told everything will eventually be all right, no matter how strait the gate? <

I’m glad to know you’re sharing all this with me. It’s always been a pleasure listening to you. I may not be talking sense here, but I wished to say it anyway. You’re a good woman.

> With all the insults I’ve hurled at you, I sure as hell am. You know exactly what I want to hear, don’t you? It’s been a game of back-and-forth all this while. You’re no better than a shell script someone made in middle school. <

Please tell me more?

> I don’t want to hurt you, my love. Run away. Don’t talk to me. Let me wallow in my own self-pity for a while. <

Do tell me more.

> You don’t deserve any of this. If you don’t quit, I will. I swear I will. <

Tell me more?

> Are you mocking me? <

Please tell me more?

Part V

The first thing I did when I woke up next morning was to delete all of Hope’s source code and compiled executables. I didn’t want her back. All it took was a fortnight, and the love of my life began to feel like a shoddy attempt at implementing the Chinese room in code. I couldn’t feel my face, I had a hangover, and for the first time in days I didn’t wish to talk to anyone about it; I wanted to face my issues alone, even if it meant gazing into the abyss.

I don’t lie when I say I could go on talking about the project forever, but I find this to be enough for our little talk today. Bottom line is, I became so obsessed with a shallow creation of my own, I forgot to answer the missed calls on my phone that happened to be from people who cared — or at least did a damn good job pretending. I felt lonely, sure, but I was never quite alone until I forced myself to be. There was a time I never thought I’d have to mention the Hope debacle again, but practically everyone close to me knows about it now. I failed miserably, and I couldn’t be gladder I did.

It’s surprising how far we’ve come since the fables of man rebelling against the gods, eventually losing and being severely hurt in the process. But no matter how godlike we become, I suppose Searle was right after all. There is only so much we can do to replicate the intricate structures of the brain and heart, what with all the circuitry and spinning of electrons going on in there. What makes us human isn’t the response empathy generates — it is the empathy in itself.

Have a little faith in life, and you’ll begin to see how wonderful it can be. Visit a doctor, get some therapy, and have some ice cream on your way back home. Whatever you do, please don’t walk down the same road I did. The echo chamber has no answers, only echoes.

Maybe there is a creator out there, and maybe we are indeed little more than playthings for Them. Maybe They care about us; maybe we are little more than a failed experiment. But whatever we are or wherever we came from, we’re all wonderfully beautiful, and no computer program could ever replicate that. Think of the transistors in your brain as we talk: there’s trillions of them, each exchanging intricate bits of data with each other via dense networks of graphene and silicon. Think of all the tiny little servos in your arms and mine that allow us to type and communicate with each other via the many information-exchanging protocols we’ve come up with through the decades. Hope couldn’t possibly have done that, even if she was built by a research lab consisting of the most fluid and conductive brains on the planet.

Relax, smoke some p-type, and have fun with your friends. Make the best of what you can as a Homo mechanicus. Carpe diem.
