Flash fiction (2022-)

Introduction

I started writing flash fiction in earnest around 2022, having experimented with it in high school, after being exhausted writing poetry and song lyrics and wanting a more suitable medium for one-off ideas and serialised stories without aesthetic limitations. I also found the medium suitable for typeset social media posts that could be produced and digested quickly, some of which can be found on my Instagram page. Here’s all the one-shots and parts of serialisations I’ve written in the genre so far (also available to read on my Medium page) in descending order of year of writing.

The Death of Rumi Saxena (2025–)

Part I (2025)

I grabbed and put on my worst jacket and left through the front door. It was an uninteresting five-minute walk to Gopal’s and I really needed my daily fix. “What’s up?” I asked as I approached the counter, catching his eye. “Fine Roll, cardamom, Jolt,” I recited like every other day — Gopal’s head bobbing in sync told me he knew it too, as did his nimble hands moving intuitively to serve loyally his hundredth customer of the day. “Anything else, boss?” he asked as he handed over my pre-roll and a tiny packet of flavoured cardamom, his gaze briefly meeting mine with a little smile out of camaraderie.

Despite living in the same neighbourhood, Gopal and I are from very different backgrounds. He’d been growing up here for years when they started building the high-rises up north — my family shifted into ours only about three years ago, around the time it became too expensive to even breathe this air. I can tell we’re both in our twenties, but my life’s much easier than his — he has a wife and schoolchild, and I couldn’t take care of a succulent from an ex — and I’m grateful for it.

I tucked the pre-roll between my darkened lips and he struck a matchstick that he carefully carried to ignite its tip. There was an empty stool inside the shop that I promptly occupied lest someone on the road recognised me — a rich haircut like mine didn’t belong in the little shops lined along the neighbourhood sewage. I extended and crossed my legs — it was one of those rare times I was the only customer at the shop — and took in a drag from the pre-roll, holding it in for a while then turning my head to the side to exhale dodging Gopal’s face; I realised a moment later he was quite into his phone screen and couldn’t care less.

The speaker on that phone was unusually loud — I could tell he was scrolling through those minute-long videos from the jarring cuts in between. I focussed on minding my own business but couldn’t — the volume was hard to ignore — and overheard ’90s Filmi dialogue and music, until the unexpected yelling of what seemed like an aggressive man began to play. “Do I look like I’m afraid of jail?” the man shouted, his incredibly intimidating tone shattering my calm perception of the environment. “I’ll beat them up, go to jail, get out of jail and beat them up again,” the looped video repeated a couple times before Gopal scrolled away to more song and dance.

“Where’s the — is the Jolt back in stock yet?” I asked quietly and smiled, trying to contain my heightened anxiety. He looked at me, nodded again, and handed over a small bottle of the energy drink from under the counter. “Feels like it’s been years!” I quipped nervously, making him chuckle a little. Feeling the pre-roll begin to burn my fingertips, I quickly undid the bottle cap and guzzled the whole two-fifty millilitres. I then put the stub into the bottle and tossed it to the drain beside me, dusted the ash off my shirt, and got up to leave.

As I walked the first couple meters back home — the nicotine pulsing through my body — I caught what I’d figured was his phone ringtone. “The buffalo is culled tonight,” Gopal answered swiftly — I can recall quite well for how odd it sounded; but really, what did I care? It was chill with Gopal. As for the money, he likely just put it on my tab, which only I knew was over what little I had left for the rest of the month.

Her and I (2024)

“I’ve never k — kissed someone before,” I said timidly.

“Oh, it’s super easy,” she chuckled. “Gently grab me by the waist and lock your lips with mine.”

And so I slowly put my hands around her petite stature and daringly pecked once on her moist lips, quickly retracting as they stretched into a smile.

“What if your parents get to know?” I asked fearfully.

“Get to know what, that we’re just two teenage girls studying together?” she said with enticing self-assurance. “Besides, if I can’t kiss a boy, who am I supposed to?”

“You’re not supposed to kiss anyone at all, s — silly!” I jested, trying to join her in play.

“Bullshit,” she declared. “For all the talk, you really think those geezers kept it in their pants?”

We laughed for a bit until my nervous gaze met her widening eyes. I could see in them the overflowing love in her heart she felt so compelled to give, and — for the first time — the loneliness that held it back. She was pretty and famous; it never crossed my mind someone like her could be lonely.

A couple minutes later we left the vacant building, exchanging glances and whispering sweet nothings on our way back to her place. It would occur to nobody in the conservative neighbourhood we were lovers — or so we hoped. It’s just a girl thing, we’d tell anyone suspicious. The men wouldn’t press and the women wouldn’t care.

A Scarlet Peace (2022–)

Part I (2022)

Out on the street, Maya honked aggressively. Ari — still looking for the phone she’d lost not fifteen minutes ago — heard but didn’t bother to respond. Shortly after Maya yelled for her the way only a mother could, the sofa let out a soft hum.

In a moment Ari stepped out of the door, slammed it shut, and ran over to sit shotgun. Wearing the seatbelt wrong, she caught her reflection in Maya’s eyes at once and abruptly turned away. Nobody said a word.

At the station, they parked the car and quickly began hauling luggage out of the trunk. Ari sulked. Maya was livid. The train engines in the distance did the shouting. Maya grabbed Ari’s bags and reluctantly followed her to the platform. The train wasn’t there yet. Maybe it should’ve stayed that way.

When the train arrived, they exchanged glances one last time. Maya parted her lips only to swallow her words. Ari dropped the sulking and grinned — making Maya crack a smile — and said goodbye, turned around, and ran away.

Maya regretted the yelling. She dragged her feet back to the car with the dented red bumper in the corner of the empty parking lot and drove it home, only to find the TV on and the sofa a mess.

Five hours later, the train blew up.

Part II (2022)

“And would the little man like something?” asked the vendor, smiling. Young Neiji pointed to the toffee jar on the register’s left. “Only if the little man brushes tonight,” chuckled Mr. Lisma from behind, patting Neiji’s shoulder, giving it a squeeze.

“That’s fifty-four,” the vendor said, handing over Mr. Lisma the receipt and Neiji the jar. The kid slid his tiny hand in and tugged at a block, but the weather had been hot and the toffee was all stuck.

“Here, I’ll take that out for you,” said the vendor softly, gently taking the jar back, yanking out a generous piece, and giving it to happy Neiji. “Got something to say, lad?” Mr. Lisma chimed in. “Thank you, miss,” Neiji uttered, as if out of habit. The lady smiled. Mr. Lisma nodded and picked up the bags, and the two walked out back into the blistering heat.

A couple minutes into waiting for the bus back home, Mr. Lisma turned to Neiji. “That woman — you see the threads around her neck?”

Neiji nodded.

“That girl was Vas scum — the worst kind,” said Mr. Lisma with contempt. “The kind that feigns kindness.”

Part III (2023)

“My name’s Uruwa,” muttered the medical student. “Dangerous name to have here in Garini,” remarked the nurse. “I’m just Nurse to you. There’s been a bomb blast and we need all hands on deck. How good are you at porter duty?”

“I’m good with orders, I suppose.”

“Then your first order is to transfer 16370 in General to A4. They’re high-priority. Change the bedpan.”

The general ward stank of sweat and spirit, every inch covered in makeshift bedding. The student pushed and shoved her way around to find the patient lost in numbers, given away only by the functional cardiogram and spotless sheets. She lifted the covers to reveal a charred, unrecognisable face — she gently lifted the body and lowered it on the wheeled stretcher and retraced her path back into the elevator.

Maya arrived at the hospital the fastest she could, cutting through the long queue of irate people anxious to see the hurt, holding up her green passport as if in proud rebellion. “Where the fuck is my daughter?” she exclaimed, as she entered the lobby repelling all security. From behind a stack of cornered, bulky monitors, an operator hushed, “Ma’am, what’s her name?”

“Ari — Ari Lisma,” dictated Maya.

The operator typed hastily.

“Ward A4, ma’am. Second floor, on the right.”

The Surgeon (2022)

Silek continued walking down the narrow corridor. On either side, the cracked concrete gasped for air. In the distance he could make out a white dress, but it lacked a face; as he got nearer he found himself attracted to what seemed like rouge, chapped lips. “The Surgeon waits for you,” they said, and the slender body stepped aside to reveal a dented door. Silek ran his hand on the metal—every single scratch arousing second thoughts.

“Knock,” blurted the lips. Silek hesitated and knocked. For a few moments—each longer than the last—there was silence, but right before he realised his soul lost, the door swung open, behind it standing an old man with a plastic grin. “Mister Silek, client number one!” greeted the man—his voice deep, his eyes empty. “Undress before I make you!”

Silek unbuttoned his shirt—the finest silk for the finest men—and removed it with trembling hands. He sat down on the slab, unzipped his pants, and slowly rolled them off his hairless legs, which he lifted over to lie down naked. The old man watched from a couple feet away, wiping his knives clean—ready to pounce.
